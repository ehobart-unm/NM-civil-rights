## New Mexico Educators

Education is a core tenet of our civil rights in New Mexico.  Without these pioneers, our education system would be lacking.  This page is dedicated to those that have made a difference by advocating for or promoting civil rights.
<p>In territorial New Mexico, public schools were instruments of learning, as in the other territories in American history, but with the effort to 'Americanize' the people of New Mexico, the curriculum was similar to that taught in American schools in the East, with a focus on places like New England and the Atlantic states.
</p>
<p>Early educators were able to change the learning environment to focus on local history, including the use of New Mexico culture and multilingual education, which was appropriate since New Mexico was the home to people speaking many different languages </p>
<p>Unfortunately, even after these changes, education was unequal for the students of New Mexico, and this continued throughout the century.  Changes have been slow to come, and equal treatment of all the people in New Mexico's history has been difficult to achieve.</p>
<p>The people profiled below all fought for Chicanx Civil Rights in the educational realm, whether it was through promoting bilingual education, teaching practical skills to rural New Mexicans, or fighting to teach a balanced history to New Mexican students.</p>

      <h4>Adelita (Nina) Otero-Warren (1881-1965)</h4>
      <p>
        Nina Otero-Warren was an educator at heart who significantly improved education in Northern New Mexico.  She dedicated twelve years to working as the Superintendent of schools in Santa Fe.  As a progressive, she wanted to promote the identity of the New Mexican people.  She did this through educating the people by using local culture.
      </p>
      <p>
      Nina also worked hard to combat problems of illiteracy and economic hardship for the people of New Mexico, and even caught the eye of President Franklin Roosevelt, who assigned her to oversee the federal literacy program in Puerto Rico.
      </p>
      <p>
      As an educator, Nina Otero-Warren was a model citizen for the state and an activist who strove to improve the lives of those living in New Mexico.
      </p>

    <div class="story-box">
      <h4>Fabiola Cabeza de Baca Gilbert (1894-1991)</h4>
      <p>
        Fabiola Cabeza de Baca was a pioneer in New Mexican education.  Much like Adelina Otero-Warren, she promoted bilingual education.  She even created her own bilingual teaching materials and taught her students using their native language - whether it was Spanish or an indigenous language.  She was fluent in Spanish, English, Tewa and Tiwa
      </p>
      <p>
      During the Great Depression years (1929-1941), she traveled to teach rural residents skills in food preservation, gardening, poultry-raising, sewing and home repair. She advocated more public services, such as running water, paved roads, electricity, and telephone service for those residents in underserved communities.
      </p>

    <div class="story-box">
      <h4>Nadine and Patsy C&oacute;rdova</h4>
      <p>
Nadine and Patsy C&oacute;rdova taught in the rural town of Vaughn, New Mexico, in the 1980s and 90s.  As members of the Chicanx community, they wanted to teach their students  (most of whom were also chicanx) lessons about the Chicanx community.  They organized a chapter of MEChA, which supports educating Chicanx people on the history of their ancestors - not just what the history books say about history on the East Coast of the US.
      </p>
      <p>
      Both sisters were fired for teaching "subversive ideas" in the classroom.  They successfully sued the district and won a monetary settlement, but the school district was never required to acknowledge whether the C&oacute;rdova sisters were victims of first amendment discrimination, since the alleged violations occurred when they teaching about Chicanx history.
      </p>
      <p>
      The lawsuit shows why the C&oacute;rdova sisters are Civil Rights activists.  This case saw teachers challenge an education system that prevented Chicanx student from learning about relevant historical experiences.  It also raised the question of who really has the authority to determine which histories are appropriate for a public school classroom?
    </p>
