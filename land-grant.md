## The Land Grant Movement was initially led by the Alianza Federal de Mercedes, which was founded by Reies Tijerina.

<ul>
  <li>This aligns with the Spanish/Mexican approach to land ownership.</li>
  <li>This centers the land grant movement within the overall Chicanx Civil Rights Movement: the people were meant to work together as a community, not as individuals.</li>
  <li>The premise behind Tijerina's claim was the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, which ended the Mexican-American War. He argued that Mexican law recognized communal landholding and that the United States failed to uphold those rights after the treaty.</li>
  <li>Importantly, the treaty did recognize property rights established under Mexican rule prior to the cession of the territory.</li>
</ul>

## How does the Land Grant Movement fit in the Civil Rights Picture?

<div class="story-box-row">

  <div class="story-box">

    <h3>Alianza Federal de Mercedes: Political and Historical Claims</h3>

    <p>
      The Alianza Federal was the most well-known organization associated
      with the twentieth-century land grant movement. Despite Reies
      Tijerina's claims and actions, the Alianza did not bring a formal
      lawsuit seeking to establish its members' ownership of the historic
      land grants.
    </p>

    <p>
      The legal status of many grants had already been the subject of
      extensive litigation, including the 1887 United States Supreme
      Court decision in the <em>Maxwell Land Grant Case</em>. However,
      that decision did not resolve every land-grant ownership dispute
      in New Mexico.
    </p>

    <p>
      Tijerina's movement challenged the historical dispossession of
      communal lands and sought recognition of the collective rights
      of land-grant communities.
    </p>

  </div>

  <div class="story-box">

    <h3>Notable Court Cases</h3>

    <p>
      In contrast to the Alianza's broader political objectives,
      <em>Apodaca v. Unknown Heirs of Following Persons Who Are
      Adjudged to Be Owners and Proprietors of the Tome Land Grant</em>
      (1982) addressed the legal rights of identifiable heirs and
      co-owners to proceeds from the sale of a particular land grant.
    </p>

    <p>
      The New Mexico Supreme Court reaffirmed that the proceeds
      from the sale of the Tome Land Grant's common lands belonged
      to the rightful heirs and co-owners, rather than to individuals
      who had received payments through an invalid corporation.
    </p>

    <p>
      The Court ruled that the district court retained authority to
      review previous distributions and require the return of
      improperly distributed proceeds. It reversed the lower court's
      decision and remanded the case for further proceedings.
    </p>

    <p>
      Although the decision did not resolve every outstanding
      land-grant dispute, it demonstrated that legal avenues remained
      available to descendants seeking recognition of their inheritance
      rights and a more equitable distribution of proceeds from lands
      that had once belonged to their communities.
    </p>

    <p>
      These cases illustrate the distinction between a broader
      movement for historical and collective justice and the
      individual legal claims that courts were prepared to adjudicate.
    </p>

  </div>

 <div class="story-box">

    <h3>Other Important Activities in the Land Grant Movement</h3>

    <p>
    Another significant legal development occurred in Colorado with <em>Lobato v. Taylor</em> (2002), a case involving descendants of settlers on the Sangre de Cristo Land Grant. In 1960, private landowner Jack Taylor purchased approximately 77,000 acres of the grant's mountainous property and subsequently restricted local residents' access to the land. The residents, whose families had traditionally used the property for grazing livestock, gathering firewood, and harvesting timber, challenged Taylor's restrictions in a lawsuit filed in 1981. In 2002, the Colorado Supreme Court recognized their rights to continue these traditional uses, relying on established property-law doctrines, including prescriptive easements, easements by estoppel, and easements arising from prior use. Although the decision did not restore communal ownership of the land, it represented an important legal victory for descendants seeking recognition of rights inherited from the original settlers. Alongside <em>Apodaca v. Unknown Heirs </em>(1982), the case demonstrates that the consequences of historic land dispossession continued to generate legal disputes decades after the height of the twentieth-century land grant movement.
    </p>

  </div>
